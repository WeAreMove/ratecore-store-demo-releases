About Fuerteventura

Fuerteventura is the second-largest of Spain’s Canary Islands and the closest to Africa, about 100 km off the coast of Morocco. It is the oldest island in the chain: millions of years of wind and sea have worn its volcanoes into low, bare hills of ochre and brown, edged by long beaches of pale sand.

The island is built for the outdoors. Sand dunes roll down to the sea at Corralejo in the north, shallow lagoons form at Sotavento in the south, and the steady trade winds and Atlantic swell have made it a centre for windsurfing, kitesurfing and surfing. The whole island has been a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve since 2009.

Fuerteventura is warm and dry all year, with highs of about 21 °C in winter and 27–28 °C in late summer. Rain is rare, and the wind keeps summer heat in check. Life outside the resorts is quiet: small villages, goat farms and the old capital, Betancuria, in the hills.

When to go to Fuerteventura

Fuerteventura is warm enough for a beach holiday in any month, with highs from about 21 °C in winter to 28 °C in August and September. The sea is warmest from August to November, at about 22–23 °C. Winter is the busiest season for winter sun, and the trade winds blow hardest in summer.

Best things to do in Fuerteventura

  1. 011. Walk the Corralejo dunes

    The Corralejo Dunes Natural Park covers a sea of pale sand dunes running down to the Grandes Playas, a string of long, wide beaches just south of Corralejo. Walk into the dunes for the views to Lobos and Lanzarote, and swim where the beach is sheltered.

  2. 022. Take the boat to Isla de Lobos

    Isla de Lobos is a small, protected volcanic islet about 15 minutes by boat from Corralejo harbour. Follow the trail around the island, climb Montaña La Caldera for the view and swim in the calm lagoon of Playa de la Concha. A free online permit from the island council is needed, and numbers are capped.

  3. 033. See Cofete beach

    Cofete is a wild, empty beach more than 10 km long, below the steep mountains of the Jandía peninsula, on the island’s far south-west. It is reached by an unpaved track from Morro Jable, by a 4x4 bus or on a tour. Swimming is dangerous: there are strong currents and no lifeguards.

  4. 044. Wade in the Sotavento lagoon

    Playa de Sotavento, near Costa Calma on the south-east coast, is a huge beach where a sandbar traps a shallow lagoon at low tide. It is one of the world’s best-known spots for windsurfing and kitesurfing, and schools along the beach teach beginners.

  5. 055. Explore the caves of Ajuy

    Ajuy is a small fishing village on the west coast where a cliff path leads to large sea caves. The rocks here are among the oldest in the Canary Islands, formed on the ocean floor long before the island rose from the sea, and are protected as a natural monument.

  6. 066. Visit Betancuria

    Betancuria, founded in 1404 in a sheltered valley in the hills, was the island’s first capital. Its whitewashed houses, the Church of Santa María and small museums make it Fuerteventura’s most historic village. Stop at the Mirador Morro Velosa on the road north for wide views over the interior.

  7. 077. Surf or learn to surf on the north coast

    The north and west coasts around El Cotillo, Lajares and Corralejo have reef and beach breaks that work best from October to March. Surf schools in Corralejo and El Cotillo run lessons for beginners on the gentler beach breaks.

  8. 088. Walk up to Calderón Hondo

    Calderón Hondo is a volcanic cone near Lajares in the north, with a deep, well-preserved crater. A marked path of about 1 hour each way from Lajares climbs to the rim, with views across the lava fields to the coast.

Hidden gems

  • Salinas del CarmenSalinas del Carmen, just south of Caleta de Fuste, is a traditional salt works on the shore with a small museum explaining how sea salt has been made on the island. Paths lead between the evaporation pans by the sea.
  • Mirador Astronómico de SicasumbreSicasumbre is a stargazing viewpoint in the hills on the road between Pájara and La Pared. Fuerteventura is a certified Starlight Reserve, and on clear nights the dark skies here show the Milky Way with the naked eye.
  • El Cotillo’s lagoonsNorth of the fishing village of El Cotillo, a string of small coves called the lagoons is sheltered by low reefs, giving calm, clear water for swimming and snorkelling, quite different from the rolling surf just to the south.

Where to stay in Fuerteventura

  • Corralejo

    Good for: First-timersNightlifeBeaches

    Corralejo is a lively former fishing town in the north, with a harbour, a pedestrian centre full of restaurants and bars, and the dunes and Grandes Playas on its doorstep. Ferries leave here for Lobos and Lanzarote.

  • Caleta de Fuste

    Good for: FamiliesQuiet stays

    Caleta de Fuste is a family resort on the east coast, about 10 minutes from the airport, built around a sheltered sandy bay and a small marina. The water is calm and the promenade is flat, which makes it easy with young children.

  • Costa Calma

    Good for: BeachesQuiet staysCouples

    Costa Calma is a spread-out resort on the south-east coast at the start of the Sotavento beaches. It is popular with windsurfers and kitesurfers and with anyone who wants long walks on sand.

  • Morro Jable and Jandía

    Good for: FamiliesBeaches

    Morro Jable is a fishing town at the southern tip of the island, with a long beach and a lighthouse, next to the resort area of Jandía. It is the base for trips to Cofete and has ferries to Gran Canaria.

  • El Cotillo

    Good for: SurfersQuiet staysFood lovers

    El Cotillo is a small, low-key fishing village on the north-west coast, known for its sunsets, surf beaches and calm lagoons. It is quieter than Corralejo, with a handful of seafood restaurants by the old harbour.

Food and drink in Fuerteventura

Fuerteventura’s food is simple Canarian cooking built on goat, fish and potatoes, with the island’s own goat’s cheese at almost every meal. Most dishes come with a choice of mojo sauces.

Dishes to try

Queso majorero
The island’s protected goat’s cheese, made mainly from the milk of the local majorera goat and sold fresh, semi-cured or cured, sometimes coated in paprika or gofio.
Papas arrugadas con mojo
Small potatoes boiled in salty water until their skins wrinkle, served with mojo rojo (a red pepper and garlic sauce) and mojo verde (a green coriander sauce).
Carne de cabra
Slow-cooked goat stew with garlic, paprika and herbs, the island’s traditional meat dish, served in village restaurants inland.
Gofio
Flour made from toasted cereals, a Canarian staple since before the Spanish conquest, kneaded with stock or stirred into soups and desserts.
Sancocho canario
Salted fish, often grouper, boiled and served with potatoes, sweet potato, mojo and gofio.
Pescado a la plancha
Fresh local fish such as vieja (parrotfish) grilled on a hot plate, best in the harbour restaurants of Corralejo, El Cotillo and Morro Jable.

What to drink

Barraquito
A layered Canarian coffee of condensed milk, liqueur, espresso, foamed milk, lemon peel and cinnamon.
Ron miel
A sweet honey rum from the Canary Islands, often offered as a small drink after a meal.
Canarian wine
Wines from neighbouring Lanzarote and Tenerife, especially dry whites from the Malvasía grape, are widely served on the island.

Culture and etiquette

Fuerteventura is relaxed and informal, with a strong rural identity rooted in goat farming and fishing. Islanders are called majoreros, and Spanish is the everyday language.

  • Say “buenos días” or “buenas tardes” when you walk into a shop or bar; it is expected.
  • Lunch is usually from about 1.30 to 3.30 pm and dinner from about 8 pm, though resort restaurants open earlier.
  • Cover up with a shirt or wrap away from the beach, and dress modestly in churches.
  • Do not take sand, stones or plants from the dunes or protected areas; it is prohibited and can be fined.
  • Stay on marked tracks when driving or walking; off-road driving damages the fragile landscape and is not allowed.

Getting to and around Fuerteventura

Airports

  • FUEFuerteventura AirportFuerteventura Airport is at El Matorral, about 5 km south of Puerto del Rosario on the east coast. Transfers take about 10 minutes to Caleta de Fuste, 35–40 minutes to Corralejo, 45 minutes to El Cotillo, about 1 hour to Costa Calma and around 1 hour 15 minutes to Morro Jable.

Getting around

Car rental
A hire car is the best way to explore, and roads are quiet and well surfaced. Spain drives on the right. Many hire contracts do not cover unpaved tracks, such as the road to Cofete, so check before you go.
Buses
Tiadhe buses link the airport and Puerto del Rosario with the main resorts. A 4x4 bus runs from Morro Jable to Cofete, with limited seats.
Ferries
Ferries from Corralejo cross to Playa Blanca on Lanzarote in about 25–35 minutes, and boats run to Isla de Lobos in about 15 minutes. Ferries from Morro Jable reach Gran Canaria.
Taxis
Taxis wait at the airport and in the main resorts and run on meters. They are a good option for short trips but add up over the island’s long distances.

Practical information

  • Entry requirements

    UK passport holders can visit Spain, including the Canary Islands, for up to 90 days in any 180-day period without a visa. Your passport must have been issued less than 10 years before you arrive and be valid for at least 3 months after the day you plan to leave. You may be asked to show a return ticket, accommodation details and enough money for your stay. The EU’s Entry/Exit System records non-EU travellers’ fingerprints and photo at the border. EU citizens can travel with a valid passport or national identity card. Check GOV.UK foreign travel advice before you travel.

  • Money

    Fuerteventura uses the euro (EUR). Cards are widely accepted in resorts, but carry some cash for village bars and small shops. Prices of some goods are slightly lower than on the Spanish mainland because of the Canary Islands’ lower sales tax.

  • Plugs and power

    Spain uses type C and F plugs with two round pins, at 230 V and 50 Hz. UK visitors need a UK-to-European adaptor.

  • Tipping

    Tipping is not expected but is appreciated. Round up in bars and taxis, and leave 5–10% in restaurants for good service.

  • Safety

    Fuerteventura is generally safe. The main danger is the sea: strong currents and big waves are common on the west and north coasts, so swim only at beaches with lifeguards and obey the flags. Do not leave valuables in hire cars at beach car parks.

  • Health

    UK travellers should carry a valid GHIC or EHIC for state healthcare, plus travel insurance with medical cover. The sun is strong all year and the breeze hides how quickly you burn, so use high-factor sun cream and wear a hat.

  • Tap water

    Most of Fuerteventura’s tap water is desalinated seawater. It is generally safe, but it tastes of minerals, and most people drink bottled water.

  • Accessibility

    Resorts such as Caleta de Fuste, Corralejo and Morro Jable have long, flat promenades, and some main beaches have boardwalks or ramps. Dunes, wild beaches such as Cofete and the volcano trails are not accessible for wheelchair users.
