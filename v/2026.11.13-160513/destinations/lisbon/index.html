About Lisbon

Lisbon is the capital of Portugal and its largest city, built over a series of steep hills on the north bank of the River Tagus, close to where it meets the Atlantic. It is the westernmost capital of mainland Europe, and much of its centre was rebuilt after the earthquake of 1755.

The city is compact enough to explore on foot, with help from yellow trams, street lifts and the metro. Old neighbourhoods such as Alfama keep a tangle of lanes below the castle, while Baixa’s grid of streets runs down to the riverside Praça do Comércio. Viewpoints called miradouros look out over red roofs to the water.

Lisbon is mild all year. Spring and autumn are warm and bright, summer is hot and busy, and winter is cool and green, with some rain. Sintra, with its palaces, and the beach towns of Cascais and Estoril are each about 40 minutes away by train, which makes Lisbon an easy base for day trips.

When to go to Lisbon

The best time to visit Lisbon is March to May or September to October, when days are warm and sunny and walking the hills is comfortable. June to August is hot, bright and busiest, with the Santo António festivities in June. Winter is mild and quiet, but expect rain on about a third of days.

Best things to do in Lisbon

  1. 011. Walk Alfama up to Castelo de São Jorge

    Alfama is Lisbon’s oldest neighbourhood, a maze of lanes, stairways and small squares that survived the 1755 earthquake. Climb through it to Castelo de São Jorge, the Moorish castle on the hilltop, for views over the city and the River Tagus.

  2. 022. See the monuments of Belém

    Belém, about 6 km west of the centre, holds the Jerónimos Monastery and Belém Tower, both UNESCO-listed masterpieces of the early 16th-century Manueline style. Belém Tower reopened in 2026 after restoration with pre-booked, timed entry. Try a warm pastel de nata while you are there.

  3. 033. Ride tram 28

    Tram 28 runs in old yellow carriages between Martim Moniz and Campo de Ourique, climbing through Graça, Alfama, Baixa, Chiado and Estrela. It is the easiest way to see the old city’s steep streets. Board early in the morning to avoid queues, and watch your belongings on board.

  4. 044. Stroll Baixa to Praça do Comércio

    Baixa is the flat, grid-planned downtown rebuilt after the 1755 earthquake. Walk down Rua Augusta and through its triumphal arch to Praça do Comércio, the vast riverside square, and ride the Santa Justa lift, built in 1902, up to Chiado.

  5. 055. Take a day trip to Sintra

    Sintra, about 40 minutes by train from Rossio station, is a UNESCO-listed landscape of forested hills, palaces and gardens. The colourful Pena Palace, the Moorish Castle and Quinta da Regaleira are the highlights. Book timed tickets ahead and start early, as the hills get busy by late morning.

  6. 066. Watch the sunset from a miradouro

    Lisbon’s hills are lined with free public viewpoints called miradouros. Miradouro de Santa Luzia and Portas do Sol look over Alfama to the river, Miradouro da Senhora do Monte is the highest, and São Pedro de Alcântara faces the castle across the valley.

  7. 077. Hear fado in Alfama or Mouraria

    Fado, Lisbon’s melancholy song form, has been on UNESCO’s list of Intangible Cultural Heritage since 2011. Small fado houses in Alfama and Mouraria serve dinner with live singing, usually from about 8 pm. The Museu do Fado in Alfama explains its history.

  8. 088. Visit the Oceanário and Parque das Nações

    The Oceanário de Lisboa, built for Expo 98 in the modern riverside district of Parque das Nações, is one of Europe’s largest aquariums, arranged around a huge central tank. Afterwards, walk the river promenade or take the cable car along the waterfront.

Hidden gems

  • Museu Nacional do AzulejoThe National Tile Museum, in the former Madre de Deus convent east of the centre, traces five centuries of Portuguese tiles. Look for the long tiled panorama of Lisbon made before the 1755 earthquake, and the gilded convent church.
  • Aqueduto das Águas LivresThe 18th-century Águas Livres Aqueduct strides across the Alcântara valley on tall stone arches, and survived the 1755 earthquake. A section can be walked across on its open days, with wide views over the city’s west side.
  • The ferry to Cacilhas and Cristo ReiA ferry from Cais do Sodré crosses the Tagus to Cacilhas in about 10 minutes, giving one of the best views of the city skyline from the water. From there, a bus climbs to Cristo Rei, the statue in Almada with a lift to a viewing platform above the 25 de Abril Bridge.

Where to stay in Lisbon

  • Alfama and Graça

    Good for: CultureCouples

    Alfama and Graça are the old hilltop neighbourhoods around the castle, full of viewpoints, small restaurants and fado houses. Streets are steep and cobbled, and some lanes are noisy on summer festival nights.

  • Parque das Nações

    Good for: FamiliesQuiet stays

    Parque das Nações is the modern riverside district built for Expo 98, with wide promenades, the Oceanário and a large shopping centre. It is quiet, flat and close to the airport, but about 20 minutes by metro from the historic centre.

  • Cascais and Estoril

    Good for: BeachesFamiliesCouples

    Cascais and Estoril are seaside towns west of Lisbon, about 40 minutes from Cais do Sodré by train. They combine town beaches, a seafront promenade and a relaxed pace with easy trips into the city.

  • Baixa and Chiado

    Good for: First-timersCultureFood lovers

    Baixa and Chiado are Lisbon’s central districts, with flat streets of shops and cafés in Baixa and elegant squares and theatres in Chiado. Metro, trams and trains are close by, making it the easiest base for a first visit.

  • Bairro Alto and Príncipe Real

    Good for: NightlifeFood lovers

    Bairro Alto is a grid of narrow streets that fills with bars and people late into the night. Next door, Príncipe Real is calmer, with a leafy square, independent shops and restaurants.

Food and drink in Lisbon

Lisbon eats well and simply: salt cod, grilled fish, pork sandwiches and custard tarts, with coffee at a counter several times a day. Neighbourhood tascas serve a short daily menu at lunchtime.

Dishes to try

Pastel de nata
A small custard tart in crisp, flaky pastry, best eaten warm with cinnamon and icing sugar. Belém is especially known for them.
Bacalhau à Brás
Shredded salt cod scrambled with egg, onion and thin fried potato, finished with olives and parsley. It is one of hundreds of Portuguese ways to cook bacalhau.
Sardinhas assadas
Charcoal-grilled sardines on bread, the smell of Lisbon in June, when the Santo António festivities fill the old neighbourhoods.
Bifana
Thin slices of pork marinated in garlic and wine, simmered and served in a soft bread roll, often with mustard.
Amêijoas à Bulhão Pato
Clams cooked with olive oil, garlic, coriander and lemon, served with bread to soak up the juices.
Caldo verde
A soup of potato and finely shredded kale, served with a slice of chouriço.

What to drink

Ginjinha
A sweet sour-cherry liqueur served in small glasses, sometimes in a chocolate cup, from tiny bars around Rossio.
Vinho verde
A light, fresh wine from northern Portugal, often slightly sparkling, that suits seafood on a warm day.
Bica
Lisbon’s name for a short, strong espresso, drunk standing at the counter.

Culture and etiquette

Lisbon is easy-going and welcoming, but it values courtesy and a slower pace. Most people in the centre speak some English; a few words of Portuguese are still appreciated.

  • Greet staff with “bom dia” (good morning) or “boa tarde” (good afternoon) before you ask for anything.
  • Stay silent while fado is being sung; talking during a song is considered rude.
  • Starters such as bread, olives and cheese placed on the table are charged if you eat them, so send them back if you do not want them.
  • Wear shoes with good grip: Lisbon’s limestone pavements (calçada) are slippery, especially when wet.
  • Dinner usually starts from about 8 pm, and many restaurants close between lunch and dinner.

Getting to and around Lisbon

Airports

  • LISLisbon Humberto Delgado AirportLisbon Airport is about 7 km north of the city centre. The metro red line runs from the terminal, with a change to reach Baixa in about 30 minutes; a taxi to the centre takes about 20 minutes outside rush hour. Cascais is about 40 minutes away by road.

Getting around

Metro, trams and lifts
Lisbon has four metro lines, plus historic trams and street lifts for the steepest hills. A rechargeable transport card, bought at metro stations, covers the metro, buses, trams and lifts.
Trains
Trains from Rossio station run to Sintra in about 40 minutes, and trains from Cais do Sodré follow the coast to Cascais in about 40 minutes.
Ferries
Commuter ferries cross the Tagus from Cais do Sodré and Terreiro do Paço to the south bank, including Cacilhas, in about 10–20 minutes.
Taxis and cars
Taxis and ride-hailing apps are widely available and reasonably priced. You do not need a car in the city; parking is hard and the old streets are narrow. Portugal drives on the right if you hire one for day trips.

Practical information

  • Entry requirements

    UK passport holders can visit Portugal for up to 90 days in any 180-day period without a visa. Your passport must have been issued less than 10 years before you arrive and be valid for at least 3 months after the day you plan to leave. The EU’s Entry/Exit System records non-EU travellers’ fingerprints and photo at the border. EU citizens can travel with a valid passport or national identity card. Check GOV.UK foreign travel advice before you travel.

  • Money

    Portugal uses the euro (EUR). Cards and contactless payment are widely accepted, but small cafés and tascas may prefer cash. Lisbon charges a tourist tax per person per night, added to your hotel bill.

  • Plugs and power

    Portugal uses type C and F plugs with two round pins, at 230 V and 50 Hz. UK visitors need a UK-to-European adaptor.

  • Tipping

    Tipping is appreciated but not expected. Round up in cafés and taxis, and leave 5–10% in restaurants for good service.

  • Safety

    Lisbon is generally safe. Pickpockets work on tram 28, at busy viewpoints and in Baixa, so keep bags closed and phones out of back pockets. Ignore street sellers offering drugs in Baixa and Bairro Alto.

  • Health

    UK travellers should carry a valid GHIC or EHIC for state healthcare, plus travel insurance with medical cover. Summer sun is strong: wear sun cream and drink plenty of water while walking the hills.

  • Tap water

    Tap water in Lisbon is safe to drink, and public drinking fountains are common in parks.

  • Accessibility

    Lisbon is hilly, with steep, cobbled streets and many steps, especially in Alfama, Graça and Bairro Alto. Baixa, the riverside and Belém are flatter, and many metro stations have lifts, though not all. Historic trams are not step-free.

Lisbon itineraries

3 days

Lisbon in 3 days

The essential Lisbon: old neighbourhoods, Belém and a day in Sintra.

  1. Day 1Baixa, Alfama and the castleStart at Praça do Comércio and walk up through Alfama to Castelo de São Jorge. Watch the sunset from Miradouro de Santa Luzia and end with a fado dinner.
  2. Day 2Belém and ChiadoSpend the morning at the Jerónimos Monastery and Belém Tower, with a pastel de nata on the way. In the afternoon, explore Chiado and Bairro Alto, then watch the evening from São Pedro de Alcântara.

7 days

Lisbon in 7 days

A week in Lisbon with time for museums, the coast and slow evenings.

  1. Day 1Baixa and ChiadoGet your bearings in Baixa, ride the Santa Justa lift and walk Chiado’s squares and cafés.
  2. Day 2Alfama, Graça and fadoRide tram 28 to Graça, walk down through Alfama via the viewpoints and the castle, and hear fado in the evening.
  3. Day 3BelémSee the Jerónimos Monastery and Belém Tower, then walk along the river towards the 25 de Abril Bridge.
  4. Day 4SintraSpend a full day in Sintra’s palaces and gardens, starting early to beat the crowds.
  5. Day 5Cascais and the coastTake the coastal train to Cascais for the beaches and seafront, and continue to Cabo da Roca if you have a car or take the bus.
  6. Day 6Tiles, aqueduct and Parque das NaçõesVisit the Museu Nacional do Azulejo in the morning, then the Oceanário and the riverside promenade at Parque das Nações.
  7. Day 7Across the riverTake the ferry to Cacilhas for the skyline view and Cristo Rei, then spend a last evening at a miradouro in the old town.
