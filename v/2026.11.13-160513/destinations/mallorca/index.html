About Mallorca

Mallorca is the largest of the Balearic Islands, in the western Mediterranean off the east coast of Spain. It has far more variety than its package-holiday reputation suggests: a Gothic cathedral city, a UNESCO-listed mountain range, farmland dotted with old stone towns and a coastline of long sandy bays and small turquoise coves.

Palma, the capital, is a proper city with a medieval old town, a busy seafront and good food. The Serra de Tramuntana runs along the north-west coast, with villages such as Valldemossa, Deià and Sóller, while the big family resorts are in the bays of Alcúdia and Pollença in the north and along the east coast.

Summers are hot, dry and busy, and the sea is warm from June to October. Spring and autumn are ideal for walking and cycling, when the mountains are green and the roads are quieter, and winter is mild and calm. Flights from the UK are short, at a little over two hours from London.

At a glance

  • Mallorca is the largest of Spain’s Balearic Islands, in the western Mediterranean off the east coast of mainland Spain.
  • Mallorca’s Serra de Tramuntana, the mountain range along its north-west coast, has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2011.
  • Mallorca is about 2 hours 15 minutes by direct flight from London, and Palma de Mallorca Airport is about 8 km east of Palma.
  • Mallorca has long-term average highs of about 29–30°C in July and August and about 15°C in January, measured in Palma.
  • Mallorca has two official languages, Spanish and Catalan, and the local form of Catalan is called Mallorquí.

When to go to Mallorca

The best time to visit Mallorca is May to June and September to October, when it is warm and sunny and the sea is good for swimming. July and August are the hottest and busiest months, with highs around 30°C. Winter is mild and quiet, and many resort hotels close from November to March.

Best things to do in Mallorca

  1. 011. See Palma Cathedral and the old town

    Palma Cathedral, known as La Seu, is a huge Gothic church begun in the 13th century on the edge of the sea, with a vast rose window and an interior partly reworked by Antoni Gaudí in the early 1900s. Afterwards, wander the lanes of Palma’s old town and the Arab Baths.

  2. 022. Ride the old train to Sóller

    The Sóller railway, opened in 1912, runs wooden carriages from Palma through the mountains and tunnels to Sóller in about an hour. From Sóller, an old tram continues down to the harbour at Port de Sóller. Trains fill up in summer, so go early or book online.

  3. 033. Visit Valldemossa and Deià

    Valldemossa and Deià are two stone villages in the Serra de Tramuntana. In Valldemossa, the Royal Charterhouse is where Frédéric Chopin and George Sand spent the winter of 1838–39, while Deià, on a hill above the sea, was the home of the poet Robert Graves.

  4. 044. Drive the Sa Calobra road

    The road down to Sa Calobra twists through hairpin bends from the mountains to a small cove on the north-west coast. From there a path leads through rock tunnels to the mouth of the Torrent de Pareis gorge. In summer, a boat from Port de Sóller is an easier way in.

  5. 055. Go out to Cap de Formentor

    Cap de Formentor is Mallorca’s northern tip, a narrow ridge of cliffs ending at a lighthouse. Stop at the Mirador des Colomer for the view. From mid-May to mid-October, private cars are restricted on the lighthouse road during the day, so take the bus from Port de Pollença.

  6. 066. See the lake inside the Caves of Drach

    The Caves of Drach (Coves del Drac), at Porto Cristo on the east coast, are a series of large limestone caves with an underground lake, Lake Martel. Visits end with a short classical music concert on the lake. Tours are timed, so book ahead in summer.

  7. 077. Spend a day on Es Trenc

    Es Trenc is a long, undeveloped white-sand beach on Mallorca’s south coast, near Colònia de Sant Jordi, backed by dunes and salt flats. It has shallow, clear water and no hotels behind it. Arrive early in summer, as car parks fill up.

  8. 088. Walk the walls of Alcúdia

    Alcúdia’s old town, in the north of the island, is ringed by medieval walls you can partly walk along. Just outside are the remains of Pollentia, the Roman city founded in the 2nd century BC. The town has a busy market on Tuesdays and Sundays.

Hidden gems

  • Ses PaïssesSes Païsses, just outside Artà, is a Talaiotic village from the Bronze and Iron Ages, with a massive stone gateway and walls among holm oaks. It is small, quiet and easy to visit in an hour.
  • Santuari de Sant SalvadorThe Santuari de Sant Salvador is a hilltop monastery near Felanitx, at about 500 m. A winding road leads up to it, and the view takes in much of the south and east of the island.
  • Cabrera National ParkCabrera is a small, protected archipelago off the south coast, reached by boat from Colònia de Sant Jordi. Day trips include its castle, a swim in a quiet bay and often a stop at the Blue Cave (Sa Cova Blava).

Where to stay in Mallorca

  • Palma

    Good for: CultureFood loversCouples

    Palma suits people who want a city stay with good restaurants, museums and shops, plus easy day trips. The old town has boutique hotels, and Portixol and Ciutat Jardí have small beaches a short walk or ride from the centre.

  • Alcúdia

    Good for: FamiliesBeachesFirst-timers

    Alcúdia, in the north, combines a walled old town with Port d’Alcúdia and a long, shallow sandy beach lined with family hotels. It is one of the best bases for young children.

  • Port de Pollença

    Good for: FamiliesWalkersQuiet stays

    Port de Pollença (Puerto Pollensa) is a calm resort on a sheltered bay, with a pine-lined seafront walk and views of the mountains. It is close to Cap de Formentor and good walking.

  • Port de Sóller

    Good for: WalkersCouplesQuiet stays

    Port de Sóller is a horseshoe-shaped harbour on the north-west coast, linked by tram to the town of Sóller. It is the best base for the Serra de Tramuntana and boat trips to Sa Calobra.

  • Cala d’Or

    Good for: CouplesBeachesFamilies

    Cala d’Or, on the south-east coast, is a resort of white low-rise buildings around a string of small coves and a marina. It is relaxed and close to the Mondragó Natural Park.

Food and drink in Mallorca

Mallorcan food is rustic and seasonal, built on pork, olive oil, bread, vegetables and almonds. Bakeries and weekly town markets are the easiest way in.

Dishes to try

Pa amb oli
Bread rubbed with ramallet tomatoes and drizzled with olive oil, topped with cheese, ham or olives. It is Mallorca’s everyday snack.
Sobrassada
A soft, spreadable cured pork sausage coloured with paprika, eaten on bread, sometimes with honey.
Ensaïmada
A light, spiral-shaped pastry made with pork lard and dusted with icing sugar, eaten for breakfast. Bakeries sell it plain or filled.
Tumbet
Layers of fried potato, aubergine and red pepper with a tomato sauce, Mallorca’s answer to ratatouille.
Frit mallorquí
A fry-up of diced lamb or pork offal with potatoes, peppers and fennel, served in bars as a traditional lunch.
Arròs brut
A soupy rice dish with meat, vegetables and spices, cooked in the mountain villages, especially in cooler months.

What to drink

Hierbas de Mallorca
An aniseed liqueur infused with local herbs, served sweet, medium or dry as a digestif.
Binissalem wine
Red and white wines from the Binissalem area in the centre of the island, often made with the local Manto Negro grape.
Palo
A dark, bittersweet liqueur flavoured with carob and gentian, often drunk with soda water as an aperitif.

Culture and etiquette

Mallorca is welcoming and used to visitors, but island life keeps its own rhythm and language. Respecting the rules on drinking and the protected coast matters here.

  • Many signs and menus are in Catalan first, and a few words such as bon dia (good morning) and gràcies (thank you) are appreciated.
  • Meals are late: lunch is usually from 13:30 and dinner from about 20:30.
  • Drinking alcohol in the street is banned in parts of Palma, Llucmajor and Calvià, including Playa de Palma and Magaluf, and fines can be high.
  • Cover your shoulders when you visit churches and monasteries, such as Lluc and the cathedral.
  • In small towns, many shops close in the afternoon and on Sundays.

Getting to and around Mallorca

Airports

  • PMIPalma de Mallorca AirportPalma de Mallorca Airport is about 8 km east of Palma. By road it takes about 15–20 minutes to Palma, 25–30 minutes to Magaluf and Santa Ponsa, about 35–40 minutes to Port de Sóller, and about 50–60 minutes to Alcúdia, Port de Pollença and Cala d’Or.

Getting around

Buses
TIB buses link Palma with towns and resorts all over the island, and the EMT city bus runs from the airport to central Palma. Services are reduced in winter.
Car rental
A hire car is the easiest way to see the mountains and the quieter beaches. Spain drives on the right, mountain roads are narrow with many bends and cyclists, and parking in Palma and popular villages fills up early in summer.
Trains and trams
The historic Sóller train runs from Palma to Sóller, with a tram on to Port de Sóller. Separate commuter trains link Palma with Inca, Sa Pobla and Manacor.
Taxis and boats
Taxis are metered and wait at the airport and town ranks. In summer, boats link Port de Sóller with Sa Calobra and run along the coasts at Alcúdia, Pollença and Cala d’Or.

Practical information

  • Entry requirements

    Mallorca is part of Spain, in the EU and the Schengen area. UK passport holders can visit for up to 90 days in any 180-day period without a visa, and your passport must have been issued less than 10 years before you arrive and be valid for at least 3 months after you leave. The EU’s Entry/Exit System records non-EU travellers’ details at the border. EU citizens can travel with a passport or national ID card. Check GOV.UK foreign travel advice before you travel.

  • Money

    Mallorca uses the euro (EUR), and cards are widely accepted. The Balearic sustainable tourism tax is charged per night on most stays and is usually paid at your accommodation.

  • Plugs and power

    Spain uses type C and F plugs with two round pins, at 230 V and 50 Hz. UK visitors need a travel adaptor.

  • Tipping

    Tipping is not expected but is appreciated. Leaving around 5–10% in restaurants for good service, or rounding up in bars and taxis, is usual.

  • Safety

    Mallorca is generally safe, with low crime. Watch for pickpockets in Palma’s old town and on busy beaches, swim where there are lifeguards and flags, and take care on mountain walks in the heat.

  • Health

    UK travellers can use a GHIC for state healthcare in Spain, but you should also take out travel insurance, as it does not cover everything. Use high-factor sun cream and carry water in summer.

  • Drinking water

    Tap water in Mallorca is treated and generally safe to drink, but it is very hard and can taste of minerals or chlorine. Most locals and visitors drink bottled water.

  • Accessibility

    Palma’s seafront promenade is long and flat, but the old town has slopes, narrow pavements and cobbles. Several larger resort beaches have walkways to the water and adapted bathing points in summer, while mountain villages are steep with many steps.

Mallorca itineraries

3 days

Mallorca in 3 days

A short break that covers Palma, the mountains and one of the island’s best beaches.

  1. Day 1PalmaVisit Palma Cathedral in the morning and wander the old town. In the late afternoon, go up to Bellver Castle for the view over the bay at sunset.
  2. Day 2Sóller and the TramuntanaTake the old train to Sóller and the tram to Port de Sóller for lunch by the harbour. Return by road through Deià and Valldemossa.
  3. Day 3Es TrencDrive south to Es Trenc for a day on the sand. Stop at the salt flats of Ses Salines on the way back.
7 days

Mallorca in 7 days

A week that circles the island, from Palma and the Tramuntana to the north, the east coast and the south.

  1. Day 1PalmaSee the cathedral, the old town and the Arab Baths. End with sunset from Bellver Castle.
  2. Day 2Valldemossa and DeiàVisit the Royal Charterhouse in Valldemossa, then drive along the coast to Deià. Walk down to the small cove of Cala Deià for a swim.
  3. Day 3Sóller and Sa CalobraRide the train to Sóller and the tram to the port. In summer, take the boat along the cliffs to Sa Calobra and the Torrent de Pareis.
  4. Day 4Lluc and PollençaCross the mountains to the monastery of Lluc, then continue to Pollença and climb the Calvari steps. Spend the evening on the seafront at Port de Pollença.
  5. Day 5Formentor and AlcúdiaTake the bus out to Cap de Formentor in the morning. In the afternoon, walk the walls of Alcúdia and the Roman ruins of Pollentia.
  6. Day 6Artà and the Caves of DrachVisit the Talaiotic village of Ses Païsses near Artà, then drive south to Porto Cristo for the Caves of Drach.
  7. Day 7Es TrencSpend a last day on the white sand of Es Trenc. Have dinner by the harbour at Colònia de Sant Jordi.
